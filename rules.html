Home

Tracks your home’s market value month by month at the annual growth rate you set (or depreciates it if negative), charges ongoing ownership costs, and calculates capital gains tax if you sell.

Rule 1 Your home’s value compounds monthly so that a full year appreciates by exactly the annual rate — 3% means 3% over 12 months.
Rule 2 Property tax accrues each month at 1/12 of the annual tax rate you set, is paid from your funding account the following month, and is tracked as its own tax line.
Rule 3 Carrying costs — maintenance (a percentage of the home’s current value) and insurance (an annual dollar amount) — are charged monthly to the funding account named in the home’s fund transfers. If none is set, the funding backstop covers them (see Fund Transfers).
Rule 4 When you sell, the gain is measured against your cost basis, and the primary-residence exclusion (IRC §121) applies automatically if the home is marked as a primary home and held for more than 24 months. Holding time counts from the home’s start date in the plan, so a home entered at the plan’s first month is treated as newly bought (see What This Engine Does Not Model). The exclusion is $250,000 filing Single or $500,000 filing jointly, and unlike tax brackets it is never indexed for inflation — Congress has held it fixed since 1997.
🏦

Mortgage

A full monthly amortization schedule. Enter your remaining balance, interest rate, and months left from your statement, and the engine splits every payment into principal and interest to the penny.

Rule 1 Monthly payments use the standard amortization formula at 1/12 of your annual rate (APR) per month, matching how lenders calculate principal and interest.
Rule 2 The engine counts down your months remaining and automatically closes the mortgage once the balance reaches $0, even if you leave the finish date blank.
Rule 3 Mortgage interest is tracked as a deductible expense and factored into whether itemizing beats your standard deduction.
💰

Working Income

Your paycheck or self-employment income. Gross pay is credited on day 1 of each month, payroll taxes and pre-tax deferrals are taken out in order, and take-home pay flows to your accounts.

Rule 1 Gross income is credited on day 1 of each month. At year-end, the full annual growth rate is applied once as a raise — unlike balances and expenses, which compound smoothly each month, salaries step up annually.
Rule 2 Social Security (FICA) and Medicare taxes are withheld automatically from each paycheck (at employee or self-employed rates), so downstream transfers work from your real net pay.
Rule 3 Federal income tax withholding is estimated from your filing status and tax bracket, just like a payroll department.
Rule 4 A paycheck never pays out more than it earns. Deductions run in real payroll order: FICA → federal income tax withholding → pre-tax 401(k) and IRA deferrals → take-home pay. If an unusual income spike makes a month’s estimated withholding exceed the paycheck, the paycheck withholds all it can and the monthly true-up collects the rest from your funding accounts. If a pre-tax deferral exceeds what is left, it is trimmed for that month and the unused cap remains available to later paychecks.
📉

Monthly Expense

Recurring living costs that grow with inflation over time, letting you test whether your plan holds up over decades.

Rule 1 Expenses are paid at the end of each month (day 30), after income and rebalance transfers have settled and before monthly investment growth is applied — so money spent on bills does not also earn that month’s market return.
Rule 2 Starting in month two, inflation compounds monthly so that a full year rises by exactly your annual inflation rate (or the expense’s own growth rate, if you set one).
Rule 3 Salaries step up once a year while expenses inflate a little each month. Over a full 12-month year, both reach their exact stated annual rate.
🏛️ ✨

Retirement Accounts

401(k) · IRA · Roth

Tax-deferred and tax-free retirement accounts, with IRS contribution caps, required minimum distributions, and source withholding built in.

Rule 1 Annual contribution limits are enforced at the household level. In 2026, filing Single allows $24,500 for 401(k) ($32,500 at age 50+) and $7,500 combined for Traditional & Roth IRA ($8,600 at 50+). Filing jointly doubles those allowances ($49,000 / $65,000 for 401(k) and $15,000 / $17,200 for IRA/Roth). Caps index forward each year with your inflation rate. Contributions must come from earned income via fund transfers; any excess stays in the paycheck and is noted as Contribution capped.
Rule 2 Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) follow SECURE Act 2.0 rules based on your plan’s birth year: age 72 if born before 1951, age 73 if born 1951–1959, and age 75 if born 1960 or later. Each year’s RMD is calculated from the prior December 31 balance using the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table, and any monthly shortfall not met by your regular withdrawals is automatically distributed to your everyday deposit account.
Rule 3 401(k) and Traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, while Roth IRA distributions are completely tax-free and have no RMDs.
Rule 4 10% federal tax is withheld at the source on every Traditional IRA or 401(k) distribution (matching the Form W-4R custodian default). The withdrawal is grossed up so a $1,000 transfer still delivers $1,000 and an extra $111 leaves the account as withholding. Roth distributions and Roth conversions are excluded — conversions move the full requested amount into your Roth IRA and settle the resulting tax via true-up. If a tax-deferred account empties mid-draw, withholding spills over to your funding backstop accounts rather than being skipped.
Rule 5 Withholding only determines when and from which account cash is collected, not your final tax bill. Monthly and annual true-ups reconcile over- and under-withholding in both directions, refunding any overpayment to your everyday deposit account. Only taxes actually paid count as withheld; any unpaid balance is flagged as Unfunded.
📊

Taxable Account (Brokerage)

Your taxable investment account. Cost basis is tracked alongside market value so capital gains on every sale are measured accurately.

Rule 1 Growth compounds monthly so that a full year earns exactly the annual return rate. Dividends (qualified and non-qualified) are taxed as earned and automatically step up your cost basis when reinvested so you are never taxed on them twice.
Rule 2 When you withdraw, any fresh deposits added in the same month come out first at $0 capital gain; remaining withdrawals reduce your cost basis proportionally so realized gains match your account’s actual appreciation share.
Rule 3 Gains realized from withdrawals are taxed at long-term capital-gains rates. Short-term rates apply only if the entire account closes within 12 months of its start date (see What This Engine Does Not Model).
💵

Cash

Checking accounts or cash on hand — your most liquid bucket for everyday inflows and expenses.

Rule 1 If you set an annual return rate, interest accrues monthly and is taxed as ordinary income.
Rule 2 No capital-gains basis is tracked — every dollar withdrawn is tax-free principal.
Rule 3 Cash is first in line for both automatic payouts and automatic deposits — leftover take-home pay, RMDs, sale proceeds, and tax refunds land here when no transfer rule specifies another account, even if the balance is $0.
🐖

Savings

High-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit — a safe bucket for emergency reserves or near-term goals with predictable interest.

Rule 1 Interest compounds monthly so that a full year earns exactly the annual rate (like a bank APY) and is taxed as ordinary income.
Rule 2 Taxed identically to Cash, but modeled as its own card so you can assign separate rates and transfer rules for checking vs. high-yield savings.
💳

Debt

Credit cards, auto loans, or student debt. Enter your balance, interest rate, and payoff timeline, and the engine builds the monthly amortization schedule.

Rule 1 Uses the same standard amortization formula as a mortgage (1/12 of APR per month), splitting each payment into principal and interest.
Rule 2 You control the months to payoff, making it easy to compare faster vs. slower payoff timelines.
Rule 3 Once the balance hits $0, interest stops accruing. Unlike mortgages (which close automatically when paid off), a debt card stays open at $0 like a paid-off line of credit.
🇺🇸

US Treasury

US government bills, notes, and bonds modeled for steady federal-taxable interest income.

Rule 1 Interest compounds monthly at the annual yield you set and is taxed as ordinary federal income.
Rule 2 Principal stays flat — no market price swings or capital gains are modeled, keeping the focus on coupon income.
Rule 3 Treasury interest counts toward your federal tax bracket, provisional income, and Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT).
🏢

Corporate Bond

Corporate fixed-income holdings modeled for predictable coupon yields, separate from US Treasuries.

Rule 1 Interest compounds monthly so that a full year earns exactly the annual yield.
Rule 2 All coupon income is taxed as ordinary income (and counts toward NIIT), with no preferential capital-gains rate.
Rule 3 Like US Treasuries, no bond price appreciation or depreciation is modeled.

Fund Transfers & Automatic Failover

Fund transfers move a percentage of one asset into another on a schedule you control — such as sending salary into a 401(k), funding living expenses from an IRA, or sweeping an account when it closes.

Recurring Transfers
Move a fixed monthly percentage of a source asset into a target account. On a paycheck, 401(k) and Traditional IRA deferrals are percentages of gross pay, while savings, brokerage, and Roth transfers are percentages of take-home pay. On an expense, the percentage is the share of that bill drawn from the named account.
On-Close Transfers
When an asset reaches its finish date (or is closed by a life event), a separate close percentage sweeps its remaining balance into the target accounts automatically. Any unallocated proceeds land in your everyday deposit account.
System Transfers
Mortgage payments, property taxes, home maintenance and insurance, RMDs, and tax settlements are handled automatically. They appear in the Flows view under Moved automatically, right beside your configured rules.
Funding Backstop & Failover Order
When no transfer covers an obligation — or when a designated funding account runs dry mid-payment — the engine automatically steps through your open everyday accounts in order: Cash → Savings → Taxable Account → US Treasury → Corporate Bond, draining across multiple accounts in a single draw if needed.

Before age 60, retirement accounts are never drawn automatically (protecting them from early-withdrawal rules). Starting at age 60 (the whole-year equivalent of 59½), once every everyday account reaches $0, failover continues into retirement accounts in tax-efficient order: Traditional IRA → 401(k) → Roth IRA (with Roth strictly last resort). If no eligible account can cover the remainder, it is recorded as Unfunded rather than quietly skipped.
Automatic Deposits & Refunds
Leftover take-home pay, RMDs, sale proceeds, tax refunds, and ACA credit payouts land in the first open everyday account in the same order (Cash → Savings → Taxable Account → US Treasury → Corporate Bond), even if its balance is $0. Automatic deposits never land in retirement accounts.
Rebalance Engine
Account-to-account transfers — such as Roth conversions, savings-to-brokerage sweeps, or extra mortgage paydowns — run on day 1 of each month right after payroll finishes, so new deposits settle before rebalancing draws from them. Any capital gains or conversion income are picked up automatically by the monthly tax true-up.

Metrics

Every asset records monthly balances, cash flows, taxes, and returns. Four core metrics are pinned so they always appear first whenever you switch metrics across the timeline, charts, visualizer, or asset cards.

💰 Net Worth
Each asset’s balance at the end of the month. Summed across all balance-sheet assets (minus mortgages and debt), this is your total Net Worth.
📊 Net Worth Change (derived)
The month-over-month change in Net Worth. Combining market growth and cash flow into one figure shows at a glance whether spending is outpacing returns.
📈 Growth
Pure market appreciation earned from the annual return rate, compounded monthly so a full year equals that exact rate. Excludes deposits and withdrawals.
💸 Cash Flow
External income — wages, Social Security, pensions, interest, and dividends — minus expenses (living costs, the full mortgage payment, maintenance, and insurance) and taxes paid. Moving money between your own accounts, or withdrawing from savings to spend, is not external income. In retirement, cash flow is normally negative because withdrawals cover the gap.
Why track Net Worth Change? Investment growth alone can be misleading in retirement — a portfolio might earn 6% while withdrawals drain 8%, leaving you with less overall. Net Worth Change gives you the bottom line: did your wealth grow or shrink this month?

Tax Rates

Simulations start from the 2026 federal tax tables for your filing status: ordinary income brackets, long-term capital-gains brackets, the standard deduction, the Social Security wage base, and retirement contribution caps. Starting at age 65, the standard deduction adds the age-65+ amount ($2,050 single or $1,650 per spouse in 2026). For tax years 2025–2028, the temporary senior deduction adds $6,000 per person aged 65+, phasing out at 6% of income above $75,000 single / $150,000 joint (and available even if you itemize). Unused deductions left after zeroing ordinary income automatically shelter long-term capital gains and qualified dividends (IRC §1(h)).

From the second simulated year onward, brackets, standard deductions, the Social Security wage base, and retirement caps are indexed forward by your inflation rate so inflation alone doesn’t push you into higher brackets over a 30-year plan. Thresholds that Congress never indexed stay fixed in nominal dollars: the primary-home sale exclusion ($250,000 / $500,000), the Net Investment Income Tax threshold ($200,000 / $250,000), Social Security provisional-income thresholds ($25,000–$34,000 / $32,000–$44,000), and the temporary senior deduction.

Annual tax true-ups, NIIT, and ACA credit reconciliations settle after year-end: mid-plan settlements book in the following January, while on a plan ending in December the final year’s settlement folds into that closing December. Reference publications used by the engine:

↗ Federal income tax rates and brackets ↗ Retirement plan contributions and taxation ↗ Topic 409 — Capital Gains and Losses ↗ Topic 503 — Deductible Taxes ↗ Topic 505 — Tax Withholding and Estimated Tax ↗ Topic 590-b — Distributions from Individual Retirement Arrangements (IRAs) ↗ Net Investment Income Tax (IRC §1411) ↗ The Premium Tax Credit (IRC §36B, Form 8962) ↗ HHS poverty guidelines ↗ Form 1040 instructions with Qualified Dividends and Capital Gains Tax Worksheet

Net Investment Income Tax

IRC §1411 · 3.8%

An extra 3.8% surtax on investment income, assessed once a year on top of regular income and capital-gains taxes. It applies to the smaller of two amounts, so a large investment gain does not automatically trigger a large surtax:

Your net investment income
Interest, qualified and non-qualified dividends, and taxable capital gains (both short- and long-term, net of any IRC §121 home-sale exclusion). Wages, Social Security, pensions, and retirement-account withdrawals are not investment income and are never directly taxed by NIIT.
How far your MAGI exceeds the threshold
$200,000 filing Single or $250,000 filing jointly, measured against Modified Adjusted Gross Income (before the standard deduction). If your MAGI stays below the threshold, NIIT is $0 no matter how much investment income you earned.
⚠️ Worth planning around
A withdrawal or Roth conversion from a Traditional IRA or 401(k) is never directly taxed by NIIT, but it raises your AGI — which can push your brokerage gains and dividends over the $200,000 / $250,000 threshold and trigger 3.8% tax on them. Tax-free Roth distributions raise neither number and never trigger NIIT.

Like real life, NIIT has no monthly withholding and is settled once a year with the annual tax true-up (skipped if under $1). It appears in the Taxes column of Your Portfolio, in Reports under Prior-Year Settlement (the year after the income was earned, or in December of the final plan year), and as its own column in the Spreadsheet.

ACA Health Insurance

IRC §36B · Premium Tax Credit

An ACA Health Insurance card models a marketplace health plan together with its federal Premium Tax Credit (Form 8962). It calculates your subsidy from the income your plan actually produces without altering your transfer rules.

Rule 1 Each month, the credit equals the benchmark premium (the second-lowest-cost silver plan for your household) minus your expected contribution, capped at the plan’s actual premium. Your expected contribution is a sliding percentage of ACA MAGI (AGI plus any untaxed Social Security benefits) — ranging from 2.10% to 9.96% in 2026 and 2.15% to 10.22% in 2027.
Rule 2 Eligibility runs from 100% to 400% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) for your household size (derived from filing status unless you set a custom size). Crossing 400% FPL by even $1 eliminates the entire credit, since the temporary enhanced subsidies expired after 2025 (in 2027 the 400% cliff is $63,840 for one person and $86,560 for two). Below 100% FPL there is no credit unless you received an advance based on an estimate of 100% FPL or higher.
Rule 3 The credit is advanced monthly to lower what your accounts pay out of pocket, based on estimated annual income: prior-year ACA MAGI if you held marketplace coverage all of last year, or otherwise the card’s Estimated Income. With neither, no advance is paid. You can lower Advance Credit % to take less upfront and reduce the chance of repaying at tax time.
Rule 4 After year-end, the credit is reconciled on the return (booked the following January, or in December of a plan’s final year): any excess advance is repaid in full with no cap from 2026 onward, and any remaining credit owed is refunded to your everyday deposit account. If a plan starts mid-year (after January), its partial first year reconciles against the card’s estimate.
Rule 5 Marketplace plans reprice by calendar year, so both the premium and benchmark step up once a year in January at the card’s growth rate (defaulting to plan inflation, though you can set a higher healthcare rate). During historical backtests and Monte Carlo runs, any growth above plan inflation is preserved on top of each year’s CPI. Beyond 2027, the poverty line indexes with inflation while expected-contribution percentages scale with excess premium growth.
Rule 6 Coverage ends at Medicare: if you leave the finish date blank, the card automatically ends in December before you turn 65. Because plan premiums are set by the marketplace, ACA cards are also exempt from Guyton-Klinger guardrail cuts and raises.

Monthly premiums and advances appear under Expenses in Month & Year Details, the Tax Year strip summarizes the annual credit, and January reconciliations appear in the Taxes column and under Prior-Year Settlement in Reports.

Filing Status

Single · Married Filing Jointly

Choosing Filing As in the Settings Row switches the entire tax engine to the matching IRS tables — it is not an end-of-year discount.

What changes
Ordinary brackets, long-term capital-gains bands, standard and age-65+ deductions, NIIT and Social Security thresholds, the primary-home exclusion, and 401(k)/IRA contribution caps all switch to their joint tables.
Household totals
The engine models one household rather than two separate tax individuals. Married filers receive double the household 401(k) and IRA contribution allowances shared across their income streams.
Modeling two earners
Add a second Working Income asset for a second spouse — just like the joint Quick Start profiles (Salary A and Salary B), each with its own growth rate and retirement date.
Matching Quick Start profiles
The welcome banner automatically shows four matching starter plans: Early Career, Mid Career, Pre-Retirement, and Retired for Single; or Young Couple, Dual Income Couple, Couple Nearing Retirement, and Retired Couple for Married Filing Jointly.
Known limit Married filers currently share a single Social Security wage base ($184,500 in 2026) across the household rather than one cap per spouse. If two spouses have combined wages above the cap, Social Security payroll tax stops slightly earlier than in real life.

Retirement Income

Social Security · Annuities

Recurring retirement benefits — primarily Social Security — that begin at your chosen claiming age and adjust with COLA over time.

Rule 1 Benefits begin monthly on the asset’s start date (such as age 62, 67, or 70). Social Security cannot start before age 62, the earliest claiming age under law — a benefit dated earlier automatically defers to January of the year you turn 62 at the entered amount, and What needs attention notes the deferral.
Rule 2 An optional annual return rate applies annual cost-of-living adjustments (COLA) at year-end so your benefit keeps pace with inflation.
Rule 3 Social Security benefits are exempt from FICA and Medicare payroll taxes and cannot be used to fund IRA or 401(k) contributions (which require earned income).
Rule 4 Nothing is withheld from Social Security by default, matching real life where Form W-4V withholding is purely voluntary. Any federal tax owed on benefits is settled from your funding accounts through the monthly and annual true-ups.
Rule 5 Social Security taxation follows the IRS provisional-income test (IRC §86): other income (including capital gains) plus half of your benefits. Below $25,000 single / $32,000 joint, 0% is taxable; up to $34,000 / $44,000, up to 50% is taxable; above that, up to 85% is taxable. Because Congress never indexed these thresholds, more of your benefit becomes taxable over long projections.
🏛️

Pension

FERS · CSRS · Military · State/Local

Defined-benefit pension payments modeled separately from Social Security, with their own COLA rate and full ordinary-income taxation.

Rule 1 Monthly pension payments begin on the asset’s start date and flow to your configured accounts (or your everyday deposit backstop).
Rule 2 The annual return rate applies annual COLA increases (for example, a 2% FERS cap), independent of Social Security COLA.
Rule 3 Pension payments are 100% taxable as ordinary income, exempt from FICA, and cannot fund IRA or 401(k) contributions. No RMDs apply.
Rule 4 10% federal tax is withheld on arrival (reflecting the Form W-4P default for periodic payments), so the deposit reaching your account is net of withholding. Any over- or under-withholding is settled at year-end.

Life Events & Phases

Your financial life unfolds in chapters. During your working years, paychecks feed savings and retirement accounts; in retirement, the flow reverses so Social Security, pensions, and portfolio withdrawals cover living expenses. Life Events capture these milestones and rewire your transfer rules automatically.

🟢 Accumulate Phase
Your earning years. Paychecks flow into 401(k), IRA, Roth, savings, and brokerage accounts according to your accumulation transfer rules.
🟠 Retire Phase
Your drawdown years. Working income closes automatically, Social Security begins (at age 62 or later), and your retirement transfer rules take over to fund living expenses.
Phase Transfers Each life event holds its own set of fund transfers. When you route “Salary → 15% to 401(k)” in Accumulate and “IRA → 100% of Living Expenses” in Retire, the engine switches seamlessly at your retirement age. Any asset without new rules in a later phase inherits its rules from the prior phase.
Asset Closing Life events can close individual assets or entire asset types when a phase begins, triggering their on-close transfers. By default, Retire closes every working income that is already active (leaving any post-retirement part-time job open). If a closed asset is later deleted, the rule waits by name — shown as (not in the plan) — and reconnects automatically if you add a matching asset back.
Color-Coded Timeline The timeline shades each phase — green for Accumulate and coral for Retire. Click any phase chip (or its dot on the timeline axis) to edit it.
Age-Driven Phases trigger by age rather than hard-coded years. On a new plan, they follow Current Age, Retirement Age, and Finish Age in the settings row. On an existing plan, changing Retirement Age shifts the Retire phase together with any salaries ending and Social Security or pensions starting on the old retirement date (never moving Social Security before age 62).
Why it matters Instead of maintaining separate “working” and “retired” spreadsheets, Life Events model your entire financial arc — from first paycheck to final withdrawal — in one continuous simulation.

Why It Did That

A number you can’t explain is a number you can’t trust. Two views explain what the rules on this page actually did during your simulation — one for the plan as a whole and one for any individual asset. Both read directly from the events the engine recorded as it ran, never recomputed afterward.

⚠️ What needs attention
A collapsible bar above your asset list, grouped into 💸 Paying for the plan and 🔧 Needs setting up. It highlights the first month the plan runs out of money, any unpaid obligations, accounts that ran dry and spilled over to a backup account, capped retirement contributions, deferred Social Security, and unmet RMDs.
📖 Rule notes on an asset
Click the 👓 icon on any asset card to inspect its results over This month, This year, and Life to date, along with plain-English notes explaining the exact rules that fired — why a Roth draw owed no tax, where taxes on untaxed income were paid, what was withheld from a pension, or why a contribution was capped.
Depletion is not an alarm Retirement accounts are meant to be spent down, so hitting $0 never raises an alarm by itself. A ⚠️ badge on an asset card specifically means an obligation went unpaid — a much rarer and more serious event.
Silence is the contract A false “you couldn’t pay your mortgage” warning is worse than saying nothing. Every check stays silent unless the engine actually logged the exact failure it describes.
Engine diagnostics A third category, 🧪 Engine diagnostics, checks the simulator’s own internal bookkeeping. It stays hidden unless you turn it on in Globals — those messages are about engine reconciliation, not your finances.

What This Engine Does Not Model

Knowing where a model stops is essential to trusting its results. Below are deliberate, verified simplifications — each noted with whether it tilts projections slightly optimistic or pessimistic.

No early-withdrawal penalty
The engine uses age 60 (whole-year rounding of 59½) to protect retirement accounts from automatic failover, tax allocation, and routing-search draws. However, if you explicitly route a withdrawal from a 401(k) or IRA before age 59½, the 10% early-withdrawal penalty (IRC §72(t)) is not charged. Optimistic for explicit early draws.
No state or local income tax
Federal taxes and property taxes are modeled; state and local income taxes (and state retirement-income exemptions) are not. Optimistic in most states.
A married household has one age
Age-based milestones — age-50 catch-up limits, age-60 failover, age-65 deductions, and RMDs — use the single age entered for the household. A couple with an age gap receives both spouses’ age-based rules at the same time. Optimistic for couples with an age gap.
A home’s holding period starts in the plan
The 24-month requirement for the primary-residence sale exclusion counts from the home’s start date in the plan, not your original purchase date. A long-owned home sold within the first two simulated years will not receive the exclusion. Pessimistic for near-term sales.
Taxable withdrawals are always long-term
Gains realized by withdrawing from a brokerage account are taxed at long-term capital-gains rates regardless of how recently the money was deposited. Short-term rates apply only if an account closes within 12 months of its start date. Optimistic for short-term trading.
One Social Security wage base per household
Married filers share a single Social Security wage cap rather than one cap per spouse, so two high earners stop paying Social Security payroll tax earlier than they would in real life. Optimistic above the cap.
No rental property income
Real estate cards represent personal residences rather than rental properties, so rental income (which counts toward NIIT) is not modeled as its own asset class yet. Optimistic for landlords.
Investment expenses are not deducted
Net investment income is measured gross — advisory fees, investment interest, and allocable state taxes are not deducted from the NIIT base. Slightly pessimistic.
No RMD excise penalty
Any unmet Required Minimum Distribution is flagged in What needs attention, but the IRS excise tax on missed RMDs is not charged. Optimistic.
No Medicaid or state premium assistance
Below 100% of the Federal Poverty Level, marketplace cards receive no Premium Tax Credit (unless an advance was paid on a 100%+ estimate). Medicaid, state subsidies, and cost-sharing reductions are not modeled. Pessimistic for low incomes.
Health premiums are not age-rated
Marketplace premiums grow at the card’s single annual rate. Real-world ACA premiums also steepen with age (roughly tripling from age 21 to 64). Optimistic unless your growth rate allows for it.
No self-employed health insurance deduction
Self-employed filers can normally deduct health premiums above the line (lowering ACA MAGI and raising credits). That deduction and its interaction with ACA credits are not modeled. Pessimistic for the self-employed.

Monte Carlo Simulation

Real markets don’t deliver steady returns every year. Monte Carlo stress-tests your plan across 1,000 randomized market paths sampled from real economic history, showing the full spread of outcomes from tough markets to strong ones.

Correlated Year Sampling
Each simulated year draws a single calendar year across all four series at once — S&P 500 returns, 10-year Treasury yields, CPI inflation, and wage growth — preserving real-world correlations like stagflation or recessions. Years before retirement keep your entered rates. Drawing starts with the first January on or after retirement, including a plan’s own first month when it is that January; a plan that starts mid-year already retired keeps those first months as entered.
Percentile Bands & Survival Rate
Results display 10th, 25th, 50th (median), 75th, and 90th percentile envelopes alongside your baseline plan (real-dollar bands deflate each trial by its own sampled inflation path). The success rate is the share of trials where net worth stays above $0 every month from retirement onward.
Optional Guardrails
Check With Guardrails to apply your Guyton-Klinger spending rules inside all 1,000 trials and see how adaptive spending improves survival and downside outcomes.
Calibrated or Historical Mode
Trials draw from FRED data covering 1970–2025 (or from your chosen Backtest year forward). Calibrated (default) keeps your own configured return and inflation rates and applies each historical year’s swing around its long-run mean. Historical uses the raw historical returns as they occurred.
Always Current & Repeatable
Simulations re-run automatically whenever you edit your plan, using a fixed deterministic seed so the exact same plan always produces the exact same numbers — when a result moves, it is because your plan changed.
Why it matters A deterministic projection shows what happens if markets deliver average returns every year. Monte Carlo shows how likely your plan is to survive real volatility — especially poor returns right around retirement.

Guyton-Klinger Guardrails

Fixed withdrawal rates ignore what markets actually do. Guyton-Klinger guardrails adapt your retirement spending automatically: if your withdrawal rate climbs too high during a downturn, spending is trimmed; if strong markets push your withdrawal rate down, spending rises.

Target Rate
Your baseline annual withdrawal rate (annual spending divided by investable assets) during retirement. Guardrail triggers are measured relative to this target.
Preservation Band
How far your actual withdrawal rate can rise above the target (as a relative percentage) before a spending cut fires — protecting your portfolio from depleting in down markets.
Prosperity Band
How far your actual withdrawal rate can drop below the target before a spending raise fires — letting you enjoy strong market performance instead of underspending.
Adjustment Amount
The percentage by which open Monthly Expenses are cut or raised each time a guardrail triggers after retirement (ACA Health Insurance premiums are exempt because marketplace prices are fixed for the year).
Dual-axis chart The Guardrails chart plots portfolio value and annual withdrawals on separate axes, marking the exact year every preservation cut or prosperity raise fired.
Per-scenario Guardrail settings are saved per scenario, so you can compare conservative and flexible withdrawal strategies side by side.

Test Bench

Spending & Routing Tests

The spending test answers “how much can this plan safely spend?” using clear, concrete limits rather than an arbitrary score: find the highest monthly spending that never misses a bill, leaves at least your target balance at the end, and survives your chosen share of simulated markets. Every simulated market draw uses a fixed seed so the same plan always produces the same answer.

One uniform dial
Every monthly expense is scaled by the same multiplier across the whole plan, including any ACA Health Insurance premium. Income, taxes, mortgage payments, transfer rules, and starting balances stay untouched. A health plan’s marketplace benchmark premium stays fixed because it reflects market pricing, not your spending choice.
Never runs out
A spending level fails if any month has an unpaid bill. This check always runs first: once bills go unpaid, ending wealth is no longer meaningful because the plan stopped paying its expenses.
Leaves at least
Ending net worth in the plan’s final month, measured in today’s dollars (matching the Outcome card). Any spending level that ends below your chosen floor is rejected.
Spending
What the plan actually pays out to live on, month by month, in today’s dollars (excluding taxes). The headline monthly number is the average across the whole plan.

How the answer is found

Find the edge The solver doubles spending until the plan first runs out (up to a cap of 8× your entered spending) and narrows in on that boundary. If your plan already runs out as entered, it steps downward until the plan stays fully funded.
Scan The engine runs 40 evenly spaced spending levels from well below your entered spending up to the edge. Those exact runs form the spending curve.
Pick the highest The solver scans the full curve and picks the highest level that satisfies every limit, then checks above it — ensuring it never gets trapped below a higher passing level if a plan’s ending balance dips and rises along the curve.
Refine It narrows the gap between the highest passing level and the first failing level above it until the bracket is tighter than $10 a month.

The simulated-markets limit

On by default By default, the answer must survive at least 90% of simulated markets. You can adjust this target anywhere from 50% to 99%, or turn it off to solve strictly on your plan’s fixed return assumptions.
How survival is measured Each candidate spending level is tested across 300 simulated markets (without guardrails, using the data mode and era selected in Simulations) to measure the share of markets where net worth never drops to $0 from retirement onward. Every level uses the exact same 300 seeded markets so differences reflect spending alone.
Starting from the ceiling The fixed-path answer is the upper ceiling because no higher spending satisfies the other limits. If that ceiling already meets your market survival target, it stands as the final answer.
Stepping down Otherwise, the solver locates a lower level that does meet the survival target and bisects the gap (up to 7 steps, stopping within 0.5% of your entered spending).
Below entered spending If your plan as entered survives fewer markets than requested, the answer will be lower than what you entered. If no tested level meets the target, the Test Bench reports that plainly.
All limits still apply The lowered level is re-checked on your fixed return path to confirm it still never runs out and still leaves at least your target ending balance.
Cached rates Because survival rates depend only on spending (not your ending-balance floor or target percentage), measured rates are cached while you explore the same plan — making follow-up clicks along the curve much faster.
Independent check Once the answer is chosen from the 300-market search, it is verified one more time across 1,000 simulated markets so you get an independent confirmation.
What “lasting” means Staying above $0 total net worth from retirement onward (including retirement accounts). Because automatic failover protects retirement accounts before age 60, a plan that runs dry early can still hold positive net worth while missing a bill — which is why “never runs out” is checked separately on your plan’s fixed path.

Market returns outlook

What shifts Pessimistic (−1%) and Optimistic (+1%) shift the annual growth rate of every stock and bond account (Brokerage, Traditional IRA, Roth IRA, 401(k), US Treasury, and Corporate Bond) by ±1% per year. Dividend yields stay unchanged so total return moves by exactly one percentage point. Moderate uses your rates as entered.
What stays fixed Inflation (and expenses), wages, Cash, Savings, real estate, mortgages, debt, pensions, and Social Security stay exactly as entered. Because inflation is unchanged, a 1% return shift is also a 1% shift in real return.
Non-destructive copy Every Test Bench run works on a temporary copy with shifted rates — applying them consistently across the spending solver, simulated markets, guardrails check, routing search, and holdings chart. Your saved plan keeps your original return rates.
In simulated markets In Calibrated mode, each market swing builds on the shifted base rate. In Historical mode, ±1% is added directly to the drawn historical stock and Treasury returns (and Corporate Bonds keep their shifted rate). Either way, the 1% shift is applied exactly once.
The “As entered” column Under Moderate returns, the first column matches the Outcome card exactly. Under Pessimistic or Optimistic returns, it shows how your entered plan performs under that shifted outlook, named in the result’s Found under line.
Changing it Choosing another outlook runs nothing. Every result found under the old one dims until you click Run or Search again, so a figure on screen always describes the outlook it was found under.

What the result reports

What stops it Explains which limit prevented spending from going higher — whether your market survival target, your ending-balance floor, or an unpaid bill. When an unpaid bill stops the plan before age 60, it also names any retirement accounts still holding funds because automatic failover protects them before 60.
Simulated markets row Runs both your entered plan and the solved plan across the same 300 seeded markets (without guardrails), reusing any survival rate already measured during the search.
Guardrails row Evaluates both columns under your current Guardrails settings from retirement onward (read-only — never alters your guardrail inputs). Guardrails are not part of the spending search itself.
Health costs at 65 Because an ACA Health Insurance card ends in December before age 65 and Medicare premiums are not added automatically, the table splits monthly spending into before 65 and from 65 and notes if no post-65 health expense is in the plan.
Saving a result Save as a scenario creates a new scenario with the solved monthly expenses (truncated down to the cent so it never overshoots the tested level) while leaving your original scenario untouched.

The routing test

The routing test asks “can this plan spend more simply by changing how money flows between accounts?” It searches alternative transfer percentages — how paychecks split across accounts and which accounts fund expenses, phase by phase — using a fixed seed so the same plan, floor, and depth always yield the same results.

How routings are ranked Every candidate routing is ranked strictly by maximum sustainable monthly spending (keeping all bills paid and meeting your ending-balance floor) — never by raw ending net worth, which can reward locking money away in accounts where it isn’t available when needed.
What it can change Whole-number transfer percentages (0%–100%) for each income’s deposit targets and each monthly expense’s funding sources, keeping every asset’s outgoing percentages at or below 100%.
What it leaves alone Home, mortgage, and debt rules; account-to-account rebalance transfers; pensions or Social Security (which cannot fund retirement accounts); and any retirement-account withdrawal in a phase starting before age 60 (locked at your entered value because the 10% early-withdrawal penalty is not charged).
Contribution limits Annual 401(k) and IRA caps are still enforced by payroll during every test run, so requesting a higher deferral percentage automatically caps at the legal limit and sends the remainder to take-home pay.
Search depth Choose how widely to search: Depth 1 runs one hill-climb from your current plan; Depth 2 adds 4 climbs starting near your plan (±30 points); Depth 3 adds 5 climbs from random routings; Depth 4 adds 10 genetic searches (50 candidates × 200 generations each) polished by a final climb.
Hill-climbing Adjusts individual percentages or shifts share between two accounts on the same income or expense in progressively finer steps (20, 10, 5, 2, then 1 point) until no single 1-point move improves spending.
Pruning After each search finishes, any tweaked percentage that doesn’t meaningfully contribute to the gain (within 0.1% of the improvement) is reverted to your original value, leaving only changes that actually matter.
Ranked list & agreement Results are sorted best-first, grouping searches that found the same routing into one row. A summary notes how many searches finished within 1% of the best; if only one search reached the top, it is marked Alone at the top so you know a deeper search may find more.
Time estimate Before starting, the Test Bench estimates total runs and wall-clock time based on your plan’s parameter count, your processor’s parallel worker count, and how fast recent runs executed on your machine.
Saving a routing Save the proposed transfer percentages as a new scenario — either keeping your current spending or paired with the solved maximum spending. Your original scenario is never overwritten, and no accounts or phases are added or removed.
Routing and markets Routings are discovered on your plan’s fixed return path so the impact of each transfer rule is clear. Because maximum spending on a fixed path leaves little cushion for bad markets, check the routing’s simulated-markets row (or set an ending-balance floor above $0) before relying on it.

Scenarios & Sharing

Build and compare multiple independent scenarios — different retirement ages, savings rates, or return assumptions — and share any plan with a single private link.

Multiple Scenarios
Each scenario stores its own assets, fund transfers, life events, settings (ages, filing status, inflation, backtest year), and guardrail parameters. Switch between them from the scenario chip in the Timeline heading.
Private Link Sharing
Share links encode your scenario inside the URL fragment (#portfolio=…), which browsers never send to a server. Recipients can choose Open to view it in memory without touching their saved plans, or Open & Save to store it as a new scenario.
Auto-Persistence
Every edit saves automatically to your browser’s local storage — your data stays on your device and picks up right where you left off.
Growth at a Glance
The timeline displays opening and ending balances in both nominal and today’s dollars, plus each phase’s annualized nominal and real growth rate (shown as a dash if a phase starts or ends at $0 or below).
